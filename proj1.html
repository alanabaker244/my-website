01 — Abstract

The Shape of
What We See

Beta decay theory predicts a smooth, broad spectrum. What a real detector measures is something else entirely — reshaped by physics that has nothing to do with the nucleus.

This report investigates how a CsI scintillation detector modifies the observed β⁻ spectrum of an Sr-90/Y-90 source, and whether a detector response model can quantitatively bridge first-principles theory and experimental measurement.

The answer: yes — but the detector effects are dramatic. The observed spectrum is governed almost entirely by instrument physics. Three mechanisms explain the discrepancy: finite energy resolution, incomplete energy deposition, and a sharp efficiency rolloff above ~514 keV. A semi-empirical model incorporating all three achieves χ²ν = 2.49 over the range 20–1200 keV.

02 — Results

Key Findings

F-01 The detector dominates the spectrum shape +
Without any detector corrections, theory predicts a broad peak near 700 keV extending to 2280 keV. The experiment shows a narrow peak at 150–200 keV with rapid falloff. This apparent contradiction is entirely explained by detector physics — not nuclear physics. An analyst ignoring these effects could draw completely wrong conclusions about isotope identity or activity.
F-02 High-energy electrons are redistributed downward +
Y-90 emits electrons from 546 keV to 2280 keV, but most escape the crystal before full energy deposition — via electron escape, backscattering, or bremsstrahlung. Each event records in a lower bin than the true emission energy. The exponential tail (λ₀ = 1.901 keV, λ₁ = 0.2001) captures this redistribution. Y-90 electrons are the primary contributors to the apparent low-energy peak.
F-03 Detection probability drops sharply above 514 keV +
Ec = 513.6 keV with n = 2.904 means detection probability falls to ~50% at 514 keV, continuing steeply above that. This is consistent with electron mean free path in CsI — higher-energy electrons travel farther and are more likely to exit before stopping. Combined with energy redistribution, Y-90 events are both shifted to lower bins and less likely to register full-energy signals.
F-04 Gaussian broadening washes out the Sr-90 endpoint +
The baseline resolution σ₀ = 85.56 keV is large relative to the Sr-90 endpoint at 546 keV. At 1000 keV, fractional broadening (σ_frac = 0.2448) alone adds ~245 keV of Gaussian width — so no distinct spectral feature is visible at the Sr-90 endpoint. This is a known property of CsI scintillators, which have relatively low light yield compared to semiconductor detectors.

05 — Procedure

Method

Step 01
Theoretical Spectra

Relativistic beta spectra for Sr-90 and Y-90 constructed using the exact Fermi function F(Z,E), solving the Dirac equation in a Coulomb potential with finite nuclear size. Combined under secular equilibrium: N_true = N_Sr + N_Y.

Step 02
Data Collection

Four 900-second source runs (Spectra 0–3) plus one background run with a CsI scintillator placed directly on the Sr-90 source to maximize solid-angle coverage. All four spectra averaged; background subtracted to produce N_exp(E).

Step 03
Response Model Construction

Three-component semi-empirical model: Gaussian resolution R_Gauss, exponential energy-loss tail R_tail, and efficiency rolloff ε(E'). Blended via f(E') = f₀ + f₁·(E'/E₀). Full convolution: N_det(E) = ∫ ε(E')·R(E,E')·N_true(E') dE'.

Step 04
Nonlinear Least Squares Fit

8 free parameters fit over 20–1200 keV using scipy.optimize.least_squares with Poisson-weighted residuals: r_i = [N_model – N_exp] / √(N_exp + 1). Model normalized to match total experimental counts per iteration.

Model Equations

Full detector convolution

N_det(E) = ∫ ε(E') · R(E,E') · N_true(E') dE'

Mixed response function

R(E,E') = [1 − f(E')] · R_Gauss + f(E') · R_tail
f(E') = f₀ + f₁·(E'/E₀)

Detection efficiency

ε(E') = 1 / [1 + (E'/E_c)ⁿ]
Gaussian Resolution

Scintillation photon statistics + electronic noise. Width σ²(E') = σ₀² + (σ_frac · E')².

Energy-Loss Tail

Electron escape, backscattering, bremsstrahlung. Scale λ(E') = λ₀ + λ₁·E' grows with energy.

Efficiency Rolloff

Sigmoid cutoff at E_c = 514 keV. Higher-E electrons more likely to exit before full deposition.

06 — Systematic Uncertainties

Limitations

SYS-01
Single Background Trial

One background spectrum vs. four source spectra — asymmetric Poisson uncertainty in subtraction. Future work: average 4+ background runs.

SYS-02
Detector Slip (Spectrum 2)

Detector briefly slipped off source during run 2, reducing solid angle and count rate. Trial retained since spectral shape remained consistent.

SYS-03
Low-Energy Mismatch (E < 100 keV)

Model underestimates counts below 100 keV. Threshold pile-up, noise events, and multiple scattering not explicitly modeled — primary driver of elevated χ²ν.

SYS-04
Semi-Empirical Response

Functional forms are physically motivated but not derived from first principles. A GEANT4 simulation would model detector geometry, crystal dimensions, and angular dependence explicitly.

SYS-05
Energy Calibration Uncertainty

Bin-to-energy conversion relies on a separate calibration. Systematic error shifts all energy values. Independent mono-energetic sources would eliminate this.

SYS-06
No Angular Dependence

Model assumes isotropic electron incidence. Angular distribution and angle-dependent escape probability not modeled — a more rigorous treatment would incorporate geometric factors.

07 — Conclusion

The Instrument
Is the Message

The experimentally observed beta spectrum of an Sr-90/Y-90 source looks nothing like its first-principles prediction. But this is not a failure of nuclear theory — it is a success of detector characterization.

Three physical processes account for the entire discrepancy: finite energy resolution, incomplete energy deposition, and a detection efficiency that drops to 50% at 514 keV. By incorporating these into a semi-empirical forward model and fitting 8 free parameters by nonlinear least squares, strong agreement is achieved over 20–1200 keV with χ²ν = 2.49.

The fitted parameters are all physically meaningful and consistent with known CsI properties. The central conclusion: the observed spectrum is governed almost entirely by detector physics. Correctly interpreting radiation measurements requires understanding — and modeling — the instrument itself.
