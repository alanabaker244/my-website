01 ; Abstract

What We Can See

Beta decay theory predicts a ideal, smooth, and broad spectrum. Detector data, especially from scintillators, show us something else entirely, bent and reshaped by the non-nuclear physics of our world.

This report describes to us how a CsI scintillation detector modifies the observed beta spectrum of a clean Sr90 chain source, and whether a detector response model can adequately bridge first-principles theory and experimental measurement as successfully as the standard practice of known-source correction.

Overall, it appears so, but the detector effects are dramatic. The unmodified spectrum observed is determined almost entirely by detector physics instead of nuclear physics.

Primarily, three mechanisms can explain the discrepancy: finite energy resolution, incomplete energy deposition, and a sharp efficiency rolloff above ~514 keV. A semi-empirical model considering all three mechanisms into the free parameters achieves χ²ν = 2.49 over the range 20–1200 keV.

02 ; Results

Key Findings

F-01 The detector dominates the spectrum shape +
Without any corrections to account for the detector, theory predicts a very broad peak starting near 700 keV extending out to 2280 keV. The experimental data actually has a narrow peak at 150–200 keV with rapid falloff. This behavior can be explained through detector phenomena, but an analyst unaware of these effects could draw conclusions about isotope identity or activity that are nearly irrelevant to the actual sample.
F-02 High-energy electrons are redistributed downward +
Y-90 emits electrons from 546 keV to 2280 keV, but most escape the crystal before full energy deposition via electron escape, backscattering, and bremsstrahlung. Each event records in a lower bin than the true emission energy. The exponential tail (λ₀ = 1.901 keV, λ₁ = 0.2001) captures this redistribution. Y-90 electrons are the primary contributors to the tall and thin low-energy peak.
F-03 Detection probability drops sharply above 514 keV +
Ec = 513.6 keV with n = 2.904 means detection probability falls to ~50% at 514 keV, continuing steeply above that. This is consistent with electron mean free path in CsI, where higher-energy electrons travel farther and are more likely to exit before depositing. Combined with energy redistribution, Y-90 events are both shifted to lower bins and less likely to register full-energy signals.
F-04 Gaussian broadening washes out the Sr-90 endpoint +
The baseline resolution σ₀ = 85.56 keV is large relative to the Sr-90 endpoint at 546 keV. At 1000 keV, fractional broadening (σ_frac = 0.2448) alone adds ~245 keV of Gaussian width, meaning that no distinct spectral feature is visible at the Sr-90 endpoint. This is a known property of CsI scintillators, which have relatively low light yield compared to semiconductor detectors.

05 — Procedure

Method

Step 01
Making the theoretical spectra

The relativistic effects in the beta spectra are constructed using the Fermi function F(Z,E), and by solving the Dirac equation in a Coulomb potential with finite nuclear size. When combined under secular equilibrium, we get that Ntrue = NSr + NY.

Step 02
The Data Collection

Four 900s source runs and a control run with a CsI scintillator placed directly on the Sr-90 source to maximize the solid-angle coverage. All four spectra were then averaged, and the background subtracted to produce Nexp(E).

Step 03
Response Model Construction

We create a three section semi-empirical model: the Gaussian resolution [RGauss], the exponential energy-loss tail [Rtail], and efficiency rolloff ε(E'). These are then blended via f(E') = f₀ + f₁·(E'/E₀) to get the full equation : Ndet(E) = ∫ ε(E') · R(E,E') · Ntrue(E') dE'.

Step 04
Nonlinear Least Squares Fit

We then create 8 free parameters to represent our sections, and fit it over 20–1200 keV using scipy.optimize.least_squares with Poisson-weighted residuals : ri = [Ni – Nexp] / √(Nexp + 1). The model is then normalized to appropriately match the total experimental counts per iteration.

Model Equations

Full detector convolution

Ndet(E) = ∫ ε(E') · R(E,E') · Ntrue(E') dE'

Mixed response functions

R(E,E') = [1 − f(E')] · RGauss + f(E') · Rtail;
f(E') = f₀ + f₁·(E'/E₀)

Detection efficiency

ε(E') = 1 / [1 + (E' / Ec)n]
Gaussian Resolution

Goverened mainly by scintillation photon statistics & electronic noise. Width of σ²(E') = σ₀² + (σfrac · E')2.

Energy-Loss Tail

Due to electron escape, backscattering, and bremsstrahlung. Scale of λ(E') = λ₀ + λ₁·E', which grows with energy.

Efficiency Rolloff

Sigmoid cutoff at EC = 514 keV. Higher energy electrons more likely to exit before full deposition.

06 — Systematic Uncertainties

Limitations

LIM-01
Single Background Trial

One background spectrum vs. four source spectra means there is an asymmetric Poisson uncertainty in subtraction. For future work, an averaged 4+ background runs would improve uncertainty.

LIM-02
Detector Error in SP 2

Detector was briefly removed from the source during run 2, reducing solid angle and count rate. Trial retained since spectral shape remained varily consistent.

LIM-03
Low-Energy Mismatch (E < 100 keV)

Model underestimates counts below 100 keV. Threshold pile-up, noise events, and multiple scattering not explicitly modeled, which are all drivers of elevated χ²ν.

LIM-04
Semi-Empirical Response

Functional forms are physically motivated but not derived from first principles. A GEANT4 simulation would model detector geometry, crystal dimensions, and angular dependence explicitly and allow for accurate extrapolation.

LIM-05
Energy Calibration Uncertainty

Bin to energy conversion relies on a separate approximation, so any minor error could shift all energy values. Independent mono-energetic sources would eliminate this.

LIM-06
No Angular Dependence

Model assumes isotropic electron incidence, so angular distribution and angle-dependent escape probability are not modeled. A more rigorous future test would incorporate these geometric factors.

07 — Conclusion

The Detector
sends the message

The experimentally observed beta spectrum of an Sr-90/Y-90 source enitrely different from its first-principles prediction. But rather than this being a failure of nuclear theory, this is a success of detector characterization.

Three physical processes account for the entire discrepancy, being finite energy resolution, incomplete energy deposition, and a detection efficiency dropdown. By incorporating these into a semi-empirical forward model and fitting 8 free parameters by nonlinear least squares, strong agreement is achieved over 20–1200 keV with χ²ν = 2.49.

The fitted parameters are all physically meaningful and consistent with known CsI properties. The central conclusionis this : the unfitted spectrum is governed almost entirely by detector physics. Correctly interpreting radiation measurements requires and understanding of the instrument of measurement itself.
